💡 Airbnb APAC Implications

The Trip.com ruling is a regulatory read-across, not just a competitor headline first OTA monopoly case — SAMR's theory of harm — platform conduct toward supply partners and pricing — is portable to any marketplace at scale in China. Two practical takeaways: (1) partner-facing terms and any parity-like behaviour deserve a fresh legal read across APAC, not only China; (2) a weakened, cash-lighter Trip.com in its home market is a genuine short-term competitive opening in Chinese outbound, where it has been the default. Expect this case to be cited by regulators in Korea and India within two quarters.
Korea is the highest-conviction supply build in the region right now 10.7M H1 · 22–23M FY target · 30M ambition — Korea has policy tailwind (visa-free Chinese groups extended into H2), demand tailwind (+21.3%), distribution push (KTO tie-ups with Thai Airways, Singapore Airlines and VietJet across TH/SG/VN), and a formalized 3-year digital-nomad visa driving long stays. Meanwhile Airbnb's 15.5% host-only fee went live for all Korean hosts on May 25 — host economics changed in the same quarter demand accelerated. Host education on repricing is time-sensitive: a fee shift absorbed during a demand boom is far cheaper than one absorbed in a downturn.
Long-stay is the segment compounding fastest — and it is structurally ours Tujia 15+ day stays +40% · KR 3yr visa — Tujia reported 7+ day stays up 30% YoY and 15+ day stays up over 40%, with Yunnan and Guizhou "travel-residency" orders each up 40%+ as families relocate elderly relatives and children to cool cities for the summer. Korea's F-1-D workation visa (3 years, $5,250/month threshold) became permanent on June 30. Hotels compete poorly on multi-week stays; whole-home does not. This is where demand shape and product shape are already aligned — it needs merchandising and length-of-stay pricing, not new inventory.
Japan's regulatory drift on minpaku is the risk to watch, and it is quietly worsening zero-day rule · ¥3,000 exit tax — The July 1 departure-tax tripling to ¥3,000 is noise; the "zero-day rule" is not. Municipalities can now effectively ban short-term rentals outright in designated zones, and mandatory ICT management adds host-side compliance cost — while licensed minpaku were simultaneously pulled into the tax-collection net. Notably, new Kyoto analysis clears Chinese visitors of primary blame for overtourism (crowding is driven by Western, Korean and domestic travellers), which weakens the political narrative underpinning restrictions. That evidence is worth surfacing in local engagement before zero-day zones get drawn.
